# Interview Notes

**Authors:** Zhannur Meirambek, Ayazhan Orynbasar

## 1. How the Discussions Were Held

We held three discussions with people who look at custom furniture and door orders from different sides: a production owner and a showroom manager of the workshop LignaTech, and a customer who ordered a door from it. The production owner has about 20 years of experience in the field. Their answers were collected in writing in October 2026, through the LignaTech owner, using the questions listed below for each role. We wrote the notes in our own words and did not change their meaning.

The participants were told what the project is about and that their names would not be recorded. Only the name of the company is mentioned. The notes are used in a university project.

The notes below are raw. Our summary and conclusions are in [02-relevance-evidence.md](02-relevance-evidence.md).

## 2. Statement Read to Each Participant

> We are two students working on a university project about promising delivery dates for custom furniture orders. We would like to ask you a few questions about your experience. It takes about 15 minutes. Your participation is voluntary and you can stop at any time. We will not write down your name, and we will use your answers only in our project. Do you agree to take part?

## 3. Questions by Role

### 3.1 Showroom manager (sales)

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| M1 | What is your role, and what do you sell? |
| M2 | How is the delivery date defined today when an order is placed? Who names it and what is it based on? |
| M3 | Do you check the current load of the workshop and the availability of materials before you promise a date? How? |
| M4 | How often is the date missed, and for which reasons (workshop load, lack of materials, breakdowns, changes of the order)? |
| M5 | What happens when a date is missed (rescheduling, discount, lost customer, extra work)? |
| M6 | What information do you miss at the moment you talk to the customer? |
| M7 | What would change if you saw an estimated date, the load of the stations and the availability of materials at once when you enter the order? What could make you not trust it? |
| M8 | Which systems do you use now (1C, Excel, CRM, ERP)? |

### 3.2 Production owner

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| P1 | What is your role, and what do you produce? |
| P2 | What stages does an item go through, and how long does each usually take? |
| P3 | Which stage is usually the bottleneck, and when? |
| P4 | How do you plan the load of the stages, and how does the sales side learn about it? |
| P5 | Which materials most often delay an order, and how long does a delivery take? |
| P6 | What unplanned events change the date most? |
| P7 | How often is the date promised by sales different from what the workshop can really do? |
| P8 | Would a date range with the main reasons at the order entry help the work? What would you need to trust it? |
| P9 | Which systems do you use for planning (1C, Excel, ERP, paper)? |

### 3.3 Customer (ordered to measure)

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| C1 | What did you order? |
| C2 | What delivery date were you given, and was it given immediately or after some calculation? |
| C3 | Was the date kept? If not, by how much was it missed and what was the explanation? |
| C4 | How important is the date compared with price and quality? |
| C5 | What did you do or feel when the date was missed? |
| C6 | Which is better for you: one exact date or a range of dates with an explanation? Why? |
| C7 | What information about the date would you like to receive at the time of the order? |

## 4. Records

Names of people are not written. Each note is the participant's own statement, not our interpretation.

### Discussion 1: showroom manager

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Role | Showroom manager |
| Company | LignaTech, workshop and showroom for custom doors and furniture |
| Date and format | October 2026, written answers collected through the owner |
| Consent | [CONFIRM: yes] |

Notes:

- M1: Manager of the LignaTech showroom. Main product is custom doors: interior, hidden and designer models, door frames, extensions, skirting and related items. Helps the customer choose model, size, design, colour and set, clarifies the order and passes it to production.
- M2: The date is set preliminarily from the type and complexity of the door, finish, size, material availability and information from production. Without one up-to-date system, the manager has to ask the technologist or the workshop and rely on the data at hand. The customer should get a date that production has confirmed, not only an approximate one.
- M3: Before confirming, the manager must check that materials and fittings are available, how many orders are in the queue and whether key stations have limits. If the data are not in one place, this is found out by calls, messages or tables, so the answer takes longer and depends on how fast colleagues reply.
- M4: The date can move because of late materials, high workshop load, equipment breakdown, a change of the order after approval, errors in measuring or documents and rework. The exact frequency must be estimated from the order history. It is important to learn about the risk of delay early, not only when the customer asks.
- M5: The manager has to contact production, find the new date, tell the customer and answer questions. This takes time from selling and serving new customers. The customer may lose trust, cancel, or ask for a discount or refund, depending on the case. If the reason and the new date are unclear, communication is harder.
- M6: Needs the current estimated date, order status, confirmation of materials, place in the queue, load of key stations and possible risks. Also needs to know who is responsible for the next stage and when an update will come if the date changes.
- M7: Would answer faster and with more confidence, give a more realistic date, disturb production less and warn customers about risks in advance. Trust would depend on current data and clear reasons: the system must show what the date is based on, when it was updated and what changed it. If statuses are out of date, the forecast cannot be trusted.
- M8: Daily work uses tables, phone and messengers for contact with production and customers. The exact list of programs has to be confirmed inside the showroom. Ideally all data about the order, approval, payment, materials, production and the date would be in one system.

### Discussion 2: production owner

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Role | Production owner |
| Company | LignaTech, workshop and showroom for custom doors and furniture |
| Experience | About 20 years in the field |
| Date and format | October 2026, written answers collected through the owner |
| Consent | [CONFIRM: yes] |

Notes:

- P1: Owner and head of LignaTech. Main line is custom doors (interior, hidden and designer models) and related items. Also has experience in making furniture to order. The customer chooses model, size, design, colour and finish.
- P2: Stages: consultation and requirements; measuring and approval of sizes; drawings and specification; check and purchase of materials; cutting and preparing parts; edge work, milling and drilling where needed; painting or finishing; assembly; quality control; packing and delivery. Time depends on model, complexity, finish, materials and the current queue. Simple items go faster, non-standard and designer doors need more time. Exact times per stage are not fixed and should be recorded from real orders.
- P3: The bottleneck changes with the mix of orders and the load. Most often it is waiting for materials and fittings, complex processing or finishing, a queue at one station, or rework after errors in sizes or changed requirements. A date is hardest to estimate when several complex orders pass the same station.
- P4: The date has to be agreed with production, considering the queue, complexity, materials and the load of key stations. If information is kept in different tables, messages or passed orally, the manager cannot quickly get a current picture. Wants one system showing the status of each order, the queue by station, materials, the responsible person and the estimated date.
- P5: Possible delays: MDF and other board materials, veneer, enamels and paints, fittings, aluminium profiles, special decorative coatings. Delivery time depends on supplier stock, colour or texture, batch size and individual orders. One fixed time for all materials should not be used; the stock and the supplier's confirmed date for each item should be taken.
- P6: Equipment breakdowns, missing material, supplier delay, changes of size or design after approval, errors in measuring and drawings, defects and rework, absence of key workers. It is important to record the cause, its effect on the date and who updates the information.
- P7: The problem appears when the manager names a date without confirming the current load and materials. No exact percentage can be named without statistics; for that, promised and actual dates of finished orders should be compared, with the reason for each deviation.
- P8: Yes. The manager and the customer should see a realistic range, the current status and the main risk factors. To trust it, the calculation must consider complexity, queue and load of stations, materials and confirmed supplier dates, technology norms and the history of orders. It must update when the order changes or a delay appears, and show why the date changed.
- P9: The main task is to collect data on orders, materials and production in one clear place. Before launch it should be recorded which tools each person really uses now: tables, messengers, CRM, accounting program or paper. Important for a future solution: simplicity, access from phone and computer, access rights, change history and the possibility to see the order status without calls to the workshop.

### Discussion 3: customer

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Role | Customer |
| What was ordered | An interior door |
| Company | LignaTech |
| Date and format | October 2026, written answers collected through the owner |
| Consent | [CONFIRM: yes] |

Notes:

- C1: Ordered an interior door.
- C2: Was told about 2–3 weeks. The exact date was given after the door was chosen and the order was calculated.
- C3: The order was a little late, by about 5 days. The explanation was a delay in the delivery of materials.
- C4: Quality comes first, then the date, then the price. If a specific date is promised, the customer wants it to be kept.
- C5: Was dissatisfied, because the customer had planned to install the door earlier. Had to change plans and wait.
- C6: A range of dates is better, for example 15–20 November. It is more realistic and allows planning the installation in advance.
- C7: Would like to know the approximate date of delivery and installation and the possible reasons for a delay, and to get notifications if the dates change.
